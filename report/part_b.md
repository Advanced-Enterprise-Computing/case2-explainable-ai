# Part B – Diagnosing the bias

## B1 – The data

Women are a clear minority: about one in five applicants (2,943 of 15,000). In the scatter plot, women ask for less salary than men with the same skill; on average they ask for 64.1k$ against 71.2k$. The other features differ only slightly: women have a little less education (2.03 vs. 2.30), skill (9.9 vs. 10.6), interview score (5.0 vs. 5.3) and experience (19.6 vs. 20.1 years). The outcome differs far more: 7 % of women were recruited against 36 % of men, and in the plot the first recruited women appear at skill 14, the first recruited men already at skill 9. This can bias a model in three ways. First, it sees few women to learn from, a representation bias (Suresh & Guttag, 2021). Second, salary and education act as proxies for sex, so even a model without `sex` can tell women apart (Barocas & Selbst, 2016; Janiesch, 2026). Third, the historic labels already favour men, so a model that imitates them learns that preference, a historical bias (Mehrabi et al., 2021; Suresh & Guttag, 2021).

## B2 – Disparate Impact

The Disparate Impact (DI) compares how often the unprivileged group receives the favourable outcome with how often the privileged group receives it (Feldman et al., 2015). Here, DI = P(recruited | woman) / P(recruited | man); in the notebook, `DI = rate_unpriv / rate_priv`. A DI of 1 means both groups are recruited at the same rate; below 1, women are disadvantaged, and above 1, men are. The most common threshold is the four-fifths rule from US employment law: a selection rate below 80 % of the highest group's rate counts as evidence of adverse impact (U.S. Equal Employment Opportunity Commission, 1978). The case sheet accepts the symmetric range 0.8 ≤ DI ≤ 1.25, since 1.25 = 1/0.8 (Janiesch, 2026). In the historic data, DI = 6.66 % / 35.70 % = 0.187: women were recruited at less than a fifth of men's rate, far below 0.8. The DI only shows that the outcomes differ, not why. It ignores differences in qualification and says nothing about how a model reaches its decisions.

## B3 – DI in AIF360

Yes, slightly: AIF360 reports 0.191 for the training set and 0.169 for the test set, against 0.187 for the whole DataFrame. The formula is the same, because AIF360's `disparate_impact()` also divides the women's recruitment rate by the men's (Bellamy et al., 2019). The difference comes from the data each value is computed on: the DataFrame DI covers all 15,000 applicants, while the two BinaryLabelDatasets hold only the 12,000 training and the 3,000 test applicants of the random 80/20 split. The test set contains only about 600 women, so a few recruited women more or less move their rate, and with it the DI, noticeably. The US guidelines behind the four-fifths rule make the same point: differences based on small numbers may not be statistically significant (U.S. Equal Employment Opportunity Commission, 1978). Each value is therefore an estimate from a sample, and the full dataset gives the most reliable one.

## B4 – SHAP explanations of the baseline

SHAP splits each prediction into one contribution per feature, starting from the average prediction (here 0.244) and adding up exactly to the applicant's probability (Lundberg & Lee, 2017; based on Shapley, 1953). A global explanation describes the model's behaviour over all applicants, a local one a single decision (Janiesch, 2025). Globally, the bar plot ranks skill first (mean |SHAP| 0.229), followed by sex (0.081), the interview (0.071) and experience (0.054). Salary matters little (0.024) and education almost not at all (0.001), because the historic rule only used education through skill, which the model already sees. The beeswarm plot shows the direction: more skill, interview points and experience raise the probability, a higher salary demand lowers it, and every woman loses probability while every man gains a little. The cohort plot shows how unequal this is: for women, sex is the most important feature of all (0.189, ahead of skill with 0.129), while for men it is minor (0.055). On average, being a woman lowers the predicted probability by 0.19, while being a man raises it by 0.06. The local waterfall plot makes this concrete for one test applicant: a woman with skill 14, interview 9 and 37 years of experience gains +0.12, +0.10 and +0.07 from these features, but loses 0.45 for her sex, which leaves her at 0.072 and rejected.
