# Part A – Comparing the explanation methods

## A2 – How the four methods differ

All four methods are post-hoc and model-agnostic: they are applied after training and need only the model's inputs and outputs, so they work for any model, including the company's logistic regression (Meza Martínez et al., 2023). They differ in the question they answer. LIME and SHAP relate each feature to the prediction and produce feature attributions ("which features mattered, and how much?"). Anchors and DiCE compare the instance with similar or slightly changed instances and produce rules ("which conditions are enough for this decision?") or counterfactual examples ("what would have to change?"). Table A2 answers the five questions for each method. Following A1, it draws on sections 2.1, 2.2, 2.4, 3.2, 5.3 and 6.2 of Meza Martínez et al. (2023). The authors compared the four methods in a loan-approval scenario. First they made the methods' outputs comparable in three design rounds with users, and then they tested them with 19 participants in a lab study with eye tracking, questionnaires and interviews.

**Table A2 – The four methods compared**

| | LIME | SHAP | Anchors | DiCE |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Local or global?** | Local | Both: local for one decision, global when averaged over many (as in B4) | Local; its coverage shows how many similar cases the rule also fits | Local |
| **Form** | Feature attributions: the weights of a simple linear model fitted around the instance (Ribeiro et al., 2016) | Feature attributions: Shapley values that add up from the base value (the average prediction) to the prediction (Lundberg & Lee, 2017) | Rule: if-then conditions that are sufficient for the prediction, with precision and coverage (Ribeiro et al., 2018) | Counterfactual examples: a few diverse, nearby versions of the instance with the opposite prediction (Mothilal et al., 2020) |
| **What it reveals** | Which feature values pushed this prediction towards which class, and how strongly compared with each other | The same in exact amounts, comparable across instances and groups, e.g. women vs. men | Which few conditions are enough to fix the prediction | What would have to change to flip the decision, i.e. where the decision boundary lies near the instance |
| **What it does not reveal** | The intercept, which carries the base rate, and how the model behaves further away from the instance | Causes: it shows what the model relies on, not what drives the outcome in reality | How much each feature matters, and the role of all features outside the rule | Why the model decided as it did, how much each feature matters, and the many other counterfactuals |
| **How to read it** | A longer bar means more influence on this one decision; it is valid only near this instance | Start at the base value; each feature moves the probability up or down until the prediction is reached | If all conditions hold, the model makes the same decision with a probability of at least the precision, whatever the other features are | "Had these values been different, the decision would have been the opposite" |
| **Used by whom** | Decision-makers checking single decisions; lay users found it easy to read | Developers and auditors (global and group views) and decision-makers; lay users need some knowledge of probabilities | Lay users who want a short reason and to predict similar cases; less useful for decision-makers, who want to know how much each feature counts | Affected persons after a rejection, to see what they could change; developers and auditors, to test whether one attribute alone flips a decision |
| **Main limitation** | Unstable because of random sampling, and counterintuitive when the classes are imbalanced | Costly to compute exactly, so usually approximated; hard to read at first and easily over-trusted | Easily read as the model's complete logic, which it is not; rules near the decision boundary can get complex | Gives no reasons; can suggest unrealistic or unfair changes unless the features it may change are restricted |

**How they compare in use.** No method suited everyone. In the study, 59 % of the participants said that an explanation's usefulness depends on who receives it, and women, for example, commented more positively on DiCE than men (Meza Martínez et al., 2023). Some participants saw LIME and SHAP as more helpful for decision-makers, such as bank employees, because both show each feature's influence compared with the others. LIME was perceived as easy to read, with the right amount of detail. However, it hides its intercept, which carries the data's 70/30 class imbalance, so some approved applications showed mostly features against approval, which frustrated participants. SHAP confused some participants at first, but once they understood it they found it very useful, not least because its base and decision probabilities gave them a reference point. In the literature the study reviews, even data scientists were found to over-trust SHAP. Anchors drew the least visual attention, and in an earlier study it let users predict the model better and faster than LIME (Ribeiro et al., 2018). Yet several participants (27 %) read its rules as the only features the system considers, and some concluded that it was not real AI or could easily be fooled; Anchors ended up the least preferred. DiCE was seen as very useful for applicants after a rejection, since they could see exactly what to change. But it did not tell them why the system decided, it sometimes proposed unrealistic changes such as a new job, and it was of little use for approved applications. Simple explanations were therefore not automatically the better ones.

**What this means for our audit.** The methods complement each other, so we use each for the audience it suits. SHAP serves the auditors, HR and the works council: its cohort plot shows that sex is the most important feature for women (mean |SHAP| 0.189), and its waterfall plot shows that her sex costs applicant #1460 0.395. DiCE serves #1460 herself, but only with `sex` fixed and with features she can change. With `sex` free, it becomes a test for discrimination, because 6 of its 7 counterfactuals simply turn her into a man (D1a). Anchors shows the study's main risk. Its rule for #1460, "experience ≤ 30 and expected salary ≤ 60.63k$", is 97 % precise and easy to read, but read as the full reason it hides that her sex was decisive (D1b). We did not use LIME; since only 30 % of our applicants were recruited, its hidden intercept could confuse readers in the same way as in the study. Because a convincing explanation can still mislead (Martens et al., 2026), we trust only findings on which several methods agree.
