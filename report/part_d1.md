# Part D – Explaining the decisions

## D1a – Counterfactuals with DiCE

Applicant #1460 is a woman with skill 16, a PhD, a perfect interview score of 10 and 21 years of experience; the current model gives her a probability of 0.390 and rejects her. A counterfactual explanation states what would have had to be different for another decision (Wachter et al., 2017), and DiCE generates several diverse ones at once (Mothilal et al., 2020). When DiCE may change `sex`, 6 of its 7 counterfactuals turn her into a man, and that alone is enough: as a man she would be hired even with skill 10 or 13 instead of 16. With `sex` fixed, all 7 counterfactuals ask for more skill and experience instead; the smallest is skill 17 and 22 years of experience. Some of them even lower her interview score to values such as 9.9 or 8.3, which do not exist on the integer scale, because the notebook declares every feature continuous. Varying the threshold (0.3, 0.5, 0.9) changed nothing, because the notebook runs DiCE in regression mode with a target range, where the threshold is not used. As advice, DiCE should only change what she can act on and may fairly be asked to change, namely skill, experience and the interview (Ustun et al., 2019). Sex is a protected attribute, and lowering the salary is no fair advice either, because women in the data already ask for 6k$ less than men with the same skill. If changing only sex flips the decision, the model treats an otherwise identical applicant differently because of her sex: it violates counterfactual fairness and discriminates directly (Kusner et al., 2017).

## D1b – Rules with Anchors

An anchor is an if-then rule that keeps the prediction in place: as long as the rule holds, the model gives the same decision with high probability (Ribeiro et al., 2018). The threshold is the minimum precision the rule must reach. With 0.7, Anchors returns "experience ≤ 30" (precision 0.72, coverage 0.76); with 0.9 and 0.95, it adds "expected salary ≤ 60.63k$" (precision 0.97, coverage 0.24), so a stricter threshold gives a narrower rule that holds for fewer applicants. The rule is correct, but it does not capture why she was rejected. SHAP shows that her sex costs her 0.395 while her experience and salary barely matter (+0.002 and +0.044, both in her favour), and DiCE shows that changing only her sex flips the decision. The anchor instead describes a group she happens to belong to: in this data, applicants who ask for little are mostly weak candidates (mean skill 6.7 against 11.7), and the model rejects 95 % of them. It can also never name sex, because Anchors cuts each feature into quartiles and, with 80 % men, the only bin for sex is "sex ≤ 1", which holds everyone. Shown alone, the rule would give her a misleading reason for her rejection.
